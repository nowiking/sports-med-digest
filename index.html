J ISAKOS · 2026-11-03 · 随机对照试验 · PMID 42976660

一句话结论：在50例拟行关节镜肩袖修复的患者中，4-6周术前肩胛稳定化预康复虽在术后6个月时VAS、ASES、SPADI、Constant及EQ-5D-5L等所有患者报告结局上均较术前显著改善，但组间比较未显示预康复的额外获益，喙突压痛亦无显著差异。

要点：两组术后各时间点均较术前显著改善；预康复组在肩胛周围肌力对称性恢复上呈有利趋势，但未转化为临床结局优势；喙突压痛发生率组间无统计学差异；样本量50例，对中小效应量的检验效能有限。

对临床的启示：该阴性结果对当前将术前预康复作为肩袖修复常规路径的做法提出质疑：在等待手术的4-6周窗口内，结构化肩胛训练可能仅维持而非提升术后功能。临床资源应优先投向术后康复或高风险亚组（如术前肩胛运动障碍显著者）。需注意单中心、样本量有限及随访仅6个月的局限，长期功能与再撕裂率仍待验证。

Conclusion: A 4-6-week preoperative scapular-focused prehabilitation program did not confer additional improvements in patient-reported outcomes or coracoid tenderness at 6 months after arthroscopic rotator cuff repair compared with standard care, despite favorable trends in periscapular strength symmetry.

Key points: Both groups improved significantly across all patient-reported outcomes; the prehabilitation group showed a favorable but non-significant trend in periscapular strength symmetry; no between-group difference in coracoid tenderness; the trial was underpowered to detect small-to-moderate effects with 50 patients.

Clinical implications: These negative findings challenge the routine adoption of preoperative scapular prehabilitation before rotator cuff repair; within a 4-6-week waiting window, structured scapular training may maintain rather than enhance postoperative function. Resources may be better directed toward postoperative rehabilitation or high-risk subgroups with marked scapular dyskinesis. Limitations include a single-center design, small sample, and only 6-month follow-up; long-term function and retear rates remain to be determined.

Combined anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) and medial collateral ligament (MCL) injuries lead to anteromedial rotatory instability (AMRI), yet their in vivo biomechanics and effects on osteoarthritis (OA) severity relative to isolated injuries remain unclear. To establish a murine AMRI model via combined ACL and MCL transection (ACLT+MCLT) and compare OA progression with isolated ACL transection (ACLT) and destabilization of the medial meniscus (DMM). Controlled laboratory study. A total of 36 eight-week-old male C57BL/6 mice were used. Twelve underwent ex vivo biomechanical testing of anterior tibial translation (ATT) at neutral position, 15° internal rotation (IR), and 15° external rotation (ER) in ACLT+MCLT versus ACLT. The remaining 24 were randomized to the sham, ACLT, DMM, and ACLT+MCLT groups and underwent 6 weeks of treadmill running. ATT was assessed via photographic and radiographic evaluations. Gait, micro-computed tomography (CT) (osteophytes, subchondral bone plate), histology (OARSI score, cartilage thickness), and immunohistochemistry (type II collagen, Aggrecan, matrix metalloproteinase 13 [MMP13]) were analyzed. Data were compared using analysis of variance (ANOVA) with post hoc tests and repeated-measures ANOVA where appropriate. ACLT+MCLT showed significantly increased ATT at 15° ER versus ACLT (photographic: 1.32 ± 0.28 vs 1.01 ± 0.33 mm; P < .05; loaded radiographic: 3.46 ± 0.78 vs 2.53 ± 0.50 mm; P < .001), confirming AMRI-like instability. After 6 weeks, ACLT+MCLT caused the most severe gait impairment, including increased stride width versus ACLT and DMM (3.46 ± 0.31 vs 2.80 ± 0.23 and 3.03 ± 0.17 cm; both P < .05) and reduced stride length versus sham and ACLT (5.05 ± 0.52 vs 6.87 ± 0.36 and 5.99 ± 0.57 cm; both P < .05). Micro-CT showed the greatest osteophyte number and subchondral bone plate thickness in ACLT+MCLT mice (15 ± 2.83 and 221 ± 36 μm, respectively; all P < .05 vs ACLT and DMM). Histologically, ACLT+MCLT had the highest OARSI score (5.75 ± 0.50) and lowest cartilage thickness (117.5 ± 15 μm). Immunohistochemistry showed reduced type II collagen and Aggrecan expression (13.80% ± 2.29% and 18.60% ± 0.92%) and increased MMP13-positive cells (77.10% ± 5.51%). Combined ACL and MCL injury created a valid murine AMRI model and induced more severe OA and dysfunction than isolated injury, confirming the detrimental effect of coupled rotational anterior instability. This model offers a preclinical tool for AMRI research; clinical translation requires human studies. The established AMRI animal model simulates the complex rotatory instability of the knee after combined ACL and MCL injuries, as observed clinically. It provides a crucial preclinical tool for evaluating the efficacy of different ligament management strategies (eg, single-stage combined reconstruction vs staged treatment) in restoring joint stability and delaying OA progression. The findings suggest that in cases of significant rotatory instability, aggressive surgical intervention to restore rotational stability may be critical to protecting cartilage and improving long-term outcomes.

Alignment effects after total knee arthroplasty (TKA) may be particularly relevant in valgus phenotypes. This study compared kinematic alignment (KA) and mechanical alignment (MA) in valgus cadaveric knees during active, muscle-loaded flexion, using the native knee as reference. Ten fresh-frozen valgus knees (arithmetic hip-knee-ankle angle [aHKA] 0.8°-6.4°) were tested in a dynamic knee rig from 30° to 130° of flexion. Each specimen was assessed sequentially in its native state and after TKA with KA and MA. Tibiofemoral and patellofemoral kinematics were analyzed using one-dimensional statistical non-parametric mapping (SnPM1D) with post hoc paired F-tests, and retropatellar contact mechanics and quadriceps force using paired SPM1D t-tests. Compared with MA, KA showed less tibial internal rotation during early flexion (p = 0.011) and less dynamic valgus deviation in deep flexion (p = 0.017), as well as a larger early-flexion retropatellar contact area (p < 0.001). Neither condition restored the native anteroposterior translation pattern. Patellar tilt differed between KA and MA (p < 0.001); KA showed greater internal tilt early in flexion and subsequently shifted toward external tilt. No significant differences between KA and MA were detected for patellar shift, peak pressure or quadriceps force. Under these experimental conditions, KA and MA produced distinct rotational, coronal and patellofemoral responses. Similarity to the native reference was outcome- and flexion-dependent. The mild-to-moderate valgus range and the simulated muscle loading preclude conclusions about clinical superiority or the optimal alignment for valgus TKA. N/A.

Carbohydrate is the principal limiting fuel for prolonged, high-intensity endurance exercise, and consensus guidelines have long capped recommended intake during exercise at approximately 90 g/h. Two developments have challenged and extended contemporary fueling practice: a shift toward carbohydrate intakes of 100 to 150 g/h in elite endurance sport, and widespread commercial availability of exogenous ketone supplements marketed as ergogenic aids. Both have outpaced the clinical evidence and have reached the competitive and recreational athletes who present to sports medicine clinicians. Narrative review of primary metabolic and performance studies, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and consensus statements published through mid-2026, identified through PubMed and reference tracing. Narrative review. Level 5. Multiple-transportable-carbohydrate formulations (glucose plus fructose) raise exogenous carbohydrate oxidation above the roughly 1 g/minute ceiling imposed by intestinal glucose transport, providing the mechanistic basis for fueling rates of ≥90 g/h. Ingesting 120 g/h increases exogenous carbohydrate oxidation, but the best-controlled studies have not demonstrated additional endogenous (liver and muscle) glycogen sparing or a consistent performance advantage, and gastrointestinal tolerability requires deliberate gut training. For exogenous ketones, an influential 2016 study reported altered substrate use and improved time-trial performance. Subsequent trials and multiple systematic reviews and meta-analyses have not confirmed an acute ergogenic effect, and several have documented gastrointestinal distress or impaired performance. The more promising and reproducible signal for ketones lies in postexercise recovery and attenuation of overreaching. Finally, athletes who restrict in-competition and training carbohydrate to pursue leanness risk low energy and carbohydrate availability, the substrate of Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (REDs). Carbohydrate intake of ≤90 g/h, built through progressive gut training, is supported by consensus guidelines and mechanistic evidence for prolonged, high-intensity endurance events; progression toward 120 g/h is reasonable in selected, well-adapted athletes competing in the longest events, with the understanding that a performance benefit >90 g/h remains unproven. Routine exogenous ketone supplementation for acute performance is not supported by current evidence; a recovery-focused application is plausible and increasingly supported but requires further study.
